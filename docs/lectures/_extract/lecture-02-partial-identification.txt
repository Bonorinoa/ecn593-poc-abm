Identification as a binary, and why that topology is wrong\nMid-century econometrics inherited from the Cowles Commission a sharp, essentially binary vocabulary for\nidentification. Koopmans (1949) separated statistical inference from observations to the distribution of the data\nfrom a second step: inference from that distribution to the parameters of structural equations. A parameter was\nidentifiable within a model if it was uniquely determined by the distribution of observables; otherwise it was not.\nRank and order conditions, exclusion restrictions, and later parametric selection corrections were all technologies\nfor crossing the line from the second category into the first.\nThat binary is the wrong topology. Between a singleton and the whole parameter space there is a continuum of\ninformative sets. An assumption menu that fails to point-identify θ can still exclude large regions of the parameter\nspace. Treating every failure of uniqueness as the same kind of failure — as \"not identified,\" hence as an\ninvitation to add restrictions until uniqueness is restored — throws away exactly the information that the data plus\nthe remaining credible assumptions contain. Tamer (2010) states the methodological point directly: identification\nmaps prior assumptions and the data to information about a parameter, and that mapping \"should not result in a\nbinary answer that consists of whether the parameter is point identified.\"\nA distant ancestor, not yet using modern language, is Frisch's (1934) confluence analysis. When several linear\nrelations hold simultaneously, ordinary regression need not recover a unique coefficient vector; the observables\nare compatible with a family of complete regression systems. The later Cowles apparatus converted that\nobservation into conditions for uniqueness. The partial-identification program converts it back into a research\nobject: characterize the set.\nTwo consequences follow, and they organize the rest of the lecture. Manski's program and the Law of Decreasing Credibility\nCharles Manski's research from the late 1980s onward is the source of the modern nonparametric language.\nAnatomy of the Selection Problem (Manski 1989) considers estimation of a regression E[y | x] when (y, x) is\nsampled randomly but y is observed only selectively. The sampling process identifies E[y | x, observed] and the\nobservation probability; it does not identify E[y | x, unobserved], and therefore does not identify E[y | x]. The\nresearcher's problem is to find prior restrictions that, combined with the data, identify the regression — or, short\nof that, bound it. One restriction studied there, and then novel, is a bound on the support of y. A bounded support\nimplies a bound on the missing conditional mean and hence a bound on E[y | x]. The other restriction, already\nmuch studied, is a separability restriction from a latent-variable selection model.\nThe companion Papers and Proceedings note (Manski 1990) carries the same logic to treatment effects. Each\nperson has a pair of potential outcomes (Y(1), Y(0)); a binary treatment D reveals Y = D Y(1) + (1 − D) Y(0).\nThe sampling process identifies the distribution of (Y, D), hence E[Y | D = 1] and E[Y | D = 0]. It does not\nidentify E[Y(1) | D = 0] or E[Y(0) | D = 1]. If outcomes are bounded, the unidentified conditional means\nnevertheless lie in a known interval, and the average treatment effect\nθ ≡ E[Y(1) − Y(0)] = E[Y(1)] − E[Y(0)]\nis therefore confined to a closed interval. That interval is a population object: it is a property of P(Y, D) and of the\nsupport restriction, not of a sample. Section 5 computes it in arithmetic.\nThe 2003 monograph Partial Identification of Probability Distributions collects the program. Prediction with\nmissing outcomes, contaminated sampling, ecological inference, response-based sampling, and treatment\nresponse are all treated by the same path: first ask what the sampling process alone identifies; then ask how the\n(set-valued) identification region shrinks when further assumptions are imposed. They are what researchers\nwho disagree about selection, exclusion restrictions, or parametric error distributions can still agree on. Point\nidentification is a possible destination, reached only by adding assumptions that some readers will not accept.\nReporting only the destination conceals the price.\n2",

Those distributional and index restrictions\nare powerful. Partial identification does not\nprohibit them. It insists that they be entered on the menu as optional, and that the no-assumption bound be shown\nfirst.\n3. The identified set as a population object\nFix a sampling process that, in the population, generates a distribution P of observables. Fix a maintained\nassumption A — a restriction on the joint distribution of observables and unobservables (potential outcomes,\nlatent indices, structural disturbances). Let Q(P, A) be the set of joint distributions Q of the complete data that (i)\ninduce the observed P on the observables and (ii) satisfy A. Let θ be a functional of Q (a mean, a treatment effect,\na counterfactual choice probability, a structural parameter).\nThe identified set for θ is\nΘ(P, A) ≡ { θ(Q) : Q in Q(P, A) }.\nThree properties of this definition are easy to miss and expensive to miss.\n1 It is a population object. P is the distribution of observables in the population, not a sample analogue.\nFinite-sample uncertainty is a different problem, taken up in the next section.\n2 It is defined relative to A. Changing the assumption changes the set. Θ(P, A′) is contained in Θ(P, A)\nwhenever A′ is stronger than A. The inclusion is the geometry of the Law of Decreasing Credibility.\n3 Observational equivalence is the primitive. Two values θ and θ′ are not distinguished by (P, A) if there\nexist Q, Q′ in Q(P, A) with θ(Q) = θ and θ(Q′) = θ′. The identified set is the set of such undistinguished\nvalues.\nWhen Θ(P, A) is a singleton, θ is point-identified under A. When Θ(P, A) equals the whole logically possible\nrange of θ (for a binary-outcome ATE, the interval [−1, 1]), the data and A are uninformative. Partial\nidentification is the intermediate case: Θ(P, A) is a proper subset of the logical range, and is not a singleton.\nSharp sets and outer sets\nA reported set Θout is an outer set (or outer bound) for θ if Θ(P, A) is contained in Θout. Outer sets are valid:\nthey do not exclude any observationally equivalent value. They are not tight. A reported set is sharp if it equals\nΘ(P, A). Sharpness is a claim about the mapping (P, A) into Θ, not about sampling. In the binary-outcome\nexample of Section 5, the interval obtained from the law of total probability and the support {0, 1} is sharp: every\npoint in the interval is attained by some joint distribution of (Y(1), Y(0), D) that rationalizes the observed P(Y,\nD).\nAt survey level, a large subsequent literature characterizes identified sets using random-set theory. Molchanov\nand Molinari (2018) and Molinari (2020) treat the collection of random variables consistent with the data and the\nmaintained assumptions as a random closed set; features of θ are then identified as features of that random set\n(Aumann expectations, support functions, selections).
